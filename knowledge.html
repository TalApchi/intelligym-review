Game intelligence in one paragraph

Coaches use "game intelligence" or "soccer IQ" for a player's ability to read the game: take in what is happening around them, predict what comes next and pick a good action in time. It draws on perception (scanning, attention), memory (keeping track of players), and decision-making under time pressure. It is a description of how players play, not a single number.

What the training tools cite as evidence

Each provider's own evidence, as it describes it, with what we noticed about its limits. This research is about those tools, not about the Soccer IQ challenges.

Be Your Best

What the provider cites
Cites a 28% average increase in scan rate after 9 weeks with a German youth national team, linking to a Sky Sports article. Also cites a 2013 study linking scanning to pass completion.
Limits
The 28% figure comes from an informal federation evaluation reported in the press; a DFB official calls it "a very simple study". The 2013 study is correlational and did not test any VR training.

BlazePod

What the provider cites
A research page with about 30 references. Studies that used BlazePod itself mainly test it as a measuring tool, for example a test-retest study with 24 young adults.
Limits
The cited BlazePod studies check that it measures reaction time reliably; none shows that training with it improves soccer performance. Homepage percentages are company statistics.

The Soccer IntelliGym

What the provider cites
VU Amsterdam study with 52 academy players from PSV and AZ: IntelliGym reports a 27% larger on-field improvement than a control group after 10 weeks.
Limits
Reported as a self-deposited pilot analysis, not a journal paper; we could not read the full report.

NeuroTracker

What the provider cites
Romeas, Guldner & Faubert (2016), peer-reviewed: 23 university players; the trained group improved passing decisions in small-sided games more than the control groups.
Limits
Small study (7 to 9 players per group) from the lab where the method originated. Dribbling and shooting did not improve. Homepage percentages are not linked to specific studies.

How to read a training claim

  1. Who ran the study?

    Research run or paid for by the company selling the product is useful, but independent replication is stronger.

  2. Was there a fair comparison?

    Look for a control group that did something similar, randomly assigned, with raters who did not know who trained.

  3. How many players, and who?

    Small groups give uncertain results. Results from pilots, other sports or adults may not carry over to young soccer players.

  4. What was measured?

    Getting better at the training task is not the same as playing better in matches. Check how on-pitch performance was measured.

  5. Is it published?

    A peer-reviewed journal paper has been checked by other scientists. A company article or a self-uploaded report has not.

  6. Are the numbers rounded up?

    "Up to 30%" usually means the best case, not the typical result.

About the Soccer IQ challenges

Our five challenges are games designed around moments from soccer games: noticing a change, tracking runners, choosing a pass, reading the picture before it disappears and timing a through ball. They have not been validated as a test, and we make no claim that playing them improves performance. The research on this page is about the training tools, not about our challenges.
